Case study 01 · Technical deep dive

Designing customer-level licensing across distributed sites

The technical problem

Most customers operated a single site, while a smaller group had multiple sites across regional data stores.

Customer agreements and purchased entitlements existed at the customer level, but product consumption occurred within individual sites. The platform did not yet define how entitlements, consumption, and restrictions should operate when one customer had multiple sites.

The challenge was to establish a licensing model that preserved the commercial agreement while operating consistently across distributed customer environments.

The trade-off

Two models were considered:

  • Give each site access to the customer’s complete set of entitlements and manage its consumption independently.
  • Treat the customer’s purchased entitlements as one shared pool, with consumption calculated and restrictions enforced across all sites.

The first model was simpler to implement, but aggregate usage across the customer’s sites could exceed what the customer had purchased.

The shared-pool model preserved the commercial meaning of the agreement but introduced greater technical complexity. Consumption needed to be calculated across regional environments, shared limits enforced across sites, and the experience needed to account for licensing information synchronizing between environments.

Executives selected the shared-pool model because it provided stronger control and a consistent customer-level licensing model.

My product role

I worked with Engineering, Data Governance, and business stakeholders to make the trade-off and its implications visible before a decision was made.

After the shared-pool model was approved, I translated the direction into product requirements covering customer-level consumption, consistent enforcement of shared limits, regional data-handling constraints, synchronization of licensing and consumption information, and the experience as information moved between environments.

Technical product lesson

A commercially coherent rule can create significant distributed-systems complexity. Technical product leadership requires exposing that complexity before a decision is made, helping business and technical stakeholders evaluate the trade-off, and translating the approved commercial model into clear and enforceable platform behaviour.

LRN · Case study 01

Build and roll out a CRM-integrated provisioning, entitlement, and licensing platform

0→1Platform strategyInfluence without authorityIncremental deliveryRollout
The problem

Provisioning on LRN’s mature platform was digital but disconnected from the CRM. After a product was sold, someone still had to manually select the corresponding platform options. Licensing was available only at a high level and did not support granular entitlement or access-control management.

Although the platform generally supported what Sales offered, customer purchases could not be translated consistently into detailed platform access, usage limits, and restrictions. This created operational complexity and allowed some customers to use products beyond their purchased entitlements, resulting in revenue leakage.

Solving this required more than connecting two existing workflows. We needed to connect CRM agreements with platform provisioning, entitlements, licenses, usage, restrictions, and access controls while accounting for the platform’s technical model and preserving Sales’ commercial flexibility.

The solution depended on alignment across Sales, Revenue Operations, Data Governance, Product, Engineering, Delivery, and Support. Each group had different priorities, constraints, decision rights, and ownership.

My product decisions

Deliver value through two parallel tracks. I separated capabilities that could be released quickly from changes requiring deeper product, data, and platform work. For existing customers, we introduced non-restrictive information and controls that made overuse visible, helping them monitor their consumption while more granular restrictions were developed. In parallel, cross-functional teams continued working on the broader licensing transformation and enforceable controls.

Include licensing from the start for new commercial offerings. Any new product or capability introduced as a Sales offering was incorporated into the entitlement and licensing model from launch. This applied to both new and existing customers, preventing new offerings from adding to the legacy licensing gap while the broader transition continued.

Define entitlement enforcement as product behaviour. I identified scenarios in which customers could exceed or game their entitlements. This required decisions about how consumption would be calculated, when restrictions would apply, how exceptions would be handled, and what information customers and administrators would see. I worked with stakeholders to secure agreement or approval before translating the selected behaviours into product requirements.

Use technical analysis to guide licensing decisions. Where platform constraints created multiple implementation options, I worked with Engineering and Data Governance to define the alternatives, dependencies, regional data considerations, and customer-experience implications. I brought recommendations to the appropriate stakeholders for agreement or executive approval, then converted the selected direction into actionable product requirements.

Distribute licensing capabilities across the appropriate product roadmaps. The solution depended on capabilities owned by multiple product areas. I identified the required dependencies and worked with peer product managers to incorporate licensing work into their roadmaps while they balanced existing commitments.

Split the rollout strategy for new and existing customers. New customers entered the automated provisioning and licensing model immediately. Existing customers first received visibility into their consumption and overuse, then transitioned to granular restrictions at renewal. This delivered value without unexpectedly restricting active customers and created a controlled point to review their agreements and entitlements.

PRODUCT REASONING
CRM-disconnected provisioning and high-level licensing controlsPROBLEM
Purchases could not be translated into enforceable access and usage limitsINSIGHT
Build a granular, CRM-integrated entitlement and licensing modelDECISION
Deliver early value through phased delivery and strategic rolloutDIRECTION
Outcome

The initiative aligned senior business stakeholders and product teams around a shared model connecting CRM agreements with platform provisioning, entitlements, licenses, usage, restrictions, and access controls.

Connecting new customers to the automated licensing model immediately closed the revenue-leakage path for that segment by aligning their platform access and usage controls with what they had purchased.

Existing customers received non-restrictive information and controls that made overuse visible, allowing them to monitor and correct their consumption while more granular restrictions were developed. The renewal-based transition strategy provided a controlled path for addressing existing-customer leakage as those restrictions became available.

Licensing new commercial offerings from launch prevented additional gaps from entering the platform. Together, phased delivery and the split rollout strategy delivered immediate value while establishing a path to stronger controls across the wider customer base.

Product lesson

Platform controls cannot be designed independently of the commercial model. An unrestricted platform may give Sales greater flexibility, but enforceable licensing requires clearer decisions about how products are defined, packaged, sold, and governed.

Strong platform strategy therefore requires commercial, product, and technical decisions to evolve together. When change spans stakeholders, product roadmaps, and existing customers, phased delivery and a deliberate rollout are essential parts of the solution rather than activities that follow implementation.

LRN · Case study 02

Change direction when the solution does not solve the problem

Product judgmentBuy vs. buildTechnical analysisReprioritizationRoadmap adjustment
The problem

Lost-sales comments identified missing native integrations as a reason prospective customers did not choose LRN. We committed to building them, intending to provide real-time or near-real-time user management.

Buy-vs.-build and technical analysis later showed that native integrations would largely duplicate existing capabilities without delivering the intended outcome.

The challenge was to step back from a committed solution and redirect the investment without losing focus on the original customer and sales problem.

My product decisions

When the analysis showed that native integrations would not solve the problem, I recommended stopping the committed work rather than continuing because it was already on the roadmap.

I adjusted the roadmap and reprioritized the investment toward SCIM integrations supporting Okta, Microsoft Azure/Entra, and OneLogin, providing a standards-based path to automated user management.

Because not every customer could adopt SCIM, we also improved bulk-load processing to provide near-real-time updates for customers dependent on file-based workflows.

Together, these approaches addressed the same need across customers with different systems and levels of technical readiness.

Outcome

We avoided further investment in native integrations that would have duplicated existing capabilities without solving the underlying customer problem.

SCIM integrations with Okta, Microsoft Azure/Entra, and OneLogin enabled real-time user management. For customers unable to adopt SCIM, improved bulk-load processing moved updates from once-daily processing to near-real-time.

The solution addressed the need surfaced through lost-sales feedback while creating a meaningful commercial opportunity. 30% of existing customers migrated to the SCIM setup, and nearly all new customers using supported identity providers subscribed to the integration.

Product lesson

Product judgment means changing direction when evidence shows that a committed solution will not deliver the intended outcome.

Stopping the wrong work is only part of the decision. Product leadership must also adjust the roadmap and redirect investment toward practical alternatives that solve the original customer and business problem.

LRN · Case study 04

Fix the source of friction, not just the symptom

Root-cause analysisProduct judgmentCross-product leadershipCustomer experience
The problem

New customer issues were reported continuously. Although individual cases appeared unrelated, they could reveal recurring problems introduced by a theme, epic, workflow, or functional area.

Issues marked as “not a product bug” could also indicate genuine friction caused by unclear UX or insufficient documentation.

Without a consistent process across product teams, systemic causes were difficult to identify and prevent.

My product decisions

I introduced a shared root-cause-analysis process across the product teams. Every fixed issue was tagged with its RCA and sub-cause.

Teams also identified the theme or epic that introduced an issue—or the affected functional area when it was unrelated to new development.

I created Jira dashboards that aggregated this data across teams, exposing recurring causes and high-friction areas that were otherwise distributed across individual tickets.

For issues marked as “not a product bug,” I established a review process to identify potential UX issues or documentation gaps.

These insights helped teams prioritize systemic improvements instead of treating every issue as an isolated defect.

PRODUCT REASONING
Individual issues obscured recurring customer frictionPROBLEM
Consistent data across teams could expose systemic patternsINSIGHT
Introduce shared RCA tagging, dashboards, and issue reviewsDECISION
Prioritize prevention and root-cause improvementsDIRECTION
Outcome

After my team demonstrated the value of the approach, 100% of product teams adopted the RCA and issue-review process.

Teams gained a shared view of why issues occurred, which changes introduced them, and where customers experienced recurring friction. Issues classified as “not a product bug” could still lead to UX, documentation, or knowledge-base improvements.

Within my product portfolio, the focus on resolving root causes and customer-experience issues resulted in a 23% year-over-year reduction in newly reported customer issues, alongside fewer customer escalations and improvements in customer satisfaction and NPS.

Product lesson

Resolving an issue helps one customer; understanding its root cause can improve the experience for everyone.

Not every product issue requires a product change. Customer friction may be addressed more effectively through documentation, knowledge bases, UX guidance, or other supporting experiences.

The goal is to understand the customer’s difficulty and address it through the most appropriate part of the overall product experience.

Pearl Technologies · Case study 07

Turn complex pay rules into a configurable product

0→1No-code platformRules engineWorkflow automation
The problem

Driver pay depended on complex rules that differed by company, driver, and individual trip—and evolved quickly with changing market conditions.

Calculations had to account for agreed compensation rates, worker classification—employee or owner-operator—employment location, route complexity, time of year, taxes, statutory holidays, hourly or mileage-based pay, and single- or team-driver trips, among other factors.

These variables could interact differently for each company, making pay processing difficult to manage and scale. The challenge was to support that complexity without creating separate product logic for every customer.

My product decisions

I led the development of a no-code pay-rule and pay-calculation engine that converted company-specific policies into configurable product capabilities.

The engine evaluated rules at the driver and trip level, combining agreed compensation rates with worker classification, location, route, time of the year, tax obligations, statutory-holiday, and other configured rules.

Rather than embed each customer’s requirements directly into the product, I separated configurable business rules from the underlying calculation process. Companies could combine rules to represent their pay models while using a shared calculation foundation.

This created a consistent product capable of supporting complex pay structures without requiring a separate implementation for every customer.

A strong no-code product does not remove complexity. It organizes interacting rules so operational teams can manage them consistently.

Pearl Technologies · Case study 08

Use ML to surface fraud before pay calculation

ML-based AIFraud detectionEfficiencyAutomation
The problem

Customers reported potential fraud that was difficult to identify through manual review.

Some hourly drivers appeared to spend less time actively driving than expected while claiming the full number of hours. In other cases, mileage-based drivers took slightly longer routes, increasing their pay without materially changing arrival times.

Trip-pay teams manually reviewed large volumes of geotagged trip data before calculating pay. This prolonged processing and resulted in staggered payments rather than completing pay at the end of the regular cycle.

My product decisions

I led the development of an ML-based fraud-detection capability within the driver-pay process.

The product evaluated geotagged trip data as soon as each trip was completed, identifying activity that differed from expected trip and route patterns.

Potential issues were surfaced to trip-pay teams for review, approval, or correction. Once a trip was finalized, it became an input to the pay-calculation process.

This shifted fraud detection earlier in the workflow, reduced broad manual review at the end of the pay cycle, and kept people responsible for final decisions.

PRODUCT REASONING
Manual trip review delayed and staggered driver payPROBLEM
Completed trip data could reveal anomalous driving patternsINSIGHT
Evaluate each trip with ML as soon as it was completedDECISION
Resolve flagged trips before pay calculationDIRECTION
Outcome

The capability surfaced potential fraud immediately after trip completion, allowing trip-pay teams to focus on cases requiring attention rather than manually reviewing every trip at the end of the pay cycle.

Finalized trip data could then move into pay calculation with fewer unresolved exceptions. This contributed to a 60% faster driver-pay calculation and processing workflow, supporting more timely completion of the pay cycle.

Product lesson

AI is most valuable when it turns complex data into timely, actionable decisions within an existing workflow.

Evaluating trips when they are completed moves exception handling closer to the source of the problem. For high-impact decisions such as pay, AI should focus human attention while preserving appropriate review and approval controls.

Clickatell · Case study 10

Bring automation into the human-led journey

AIAgent productivityHuman-in-the-loopJourney orchestration
The problem

Clickatell already sold chatbots as a separate, fully automated product without human-agent involvement. Meanwhile, human agents continued handling routine follow-on requests that existing chatbot workflows could complete.

For example, after resolving a billing issue, an agent might learn that the customer also wanted to top up their mobile account or make a payment. Completing these actions manually consumed additional agent time.

The opportunity was to connect the two products so automation could increase agent capacity without fragmenting the customer journey or removing human oversight.

My product decisions

I integrated the existing chatbot product into the human-agent experience and enabled agents to trigger preconfigured automated workflows during a live conversation.

For suitable requests, such as a mobile top-up or payment, the agent could hand control to the chatbot to complete the action and then receive the conversation back.

The agent could track the workflow and take back control if the customer asked a question or needed assistance. This preserved continuity and human accountability while allowing automation to handle repeatable work.

PRODUCT REASONING
Chatbots and human-agent support operated as separate experiencesPROBLEM
Existing automated workflows could extend agent capacityINSIGHT
Let agents trigger, monitor, and interrupt chatbot workflowsDECISION
Combine automation and human support in one journeyDIRECTION
Outcome

Human agents could support more customers by delegating appropriate actions to existing chatbot workflows within the same conversation, while retaining visibility and the ability to intervene.

The connected handoff model shifted approximately 20% of agent workload to bots, while preserving customer continuity and human control. It also extended the value of Clickatell’s existing chatbot product by integrating automation into human-led support journeys.

Product lesson

Existing capabilities can create new value when they are connected across product boundaries.

Effective human-in-the-loop automation gives people control over when automation is used, visibility into its progress, and the ability to intervene when customer needs change.
